01 The one idea behind all of it

Price is what it trades at today. Value is what the business is actually worth. They drift apart — that gap is the whole game.

Everything below is just different ways of asking one question: "Am I paying more than this business is worth?" A stock can go up for reasons that have nothing to do with the business getting better (hype, short squeezes, sector momentum). When price runs far ahead of value, you trim — sell a slice, lock in gains, keep the rest — instead of selling everything or doing nothing.

The mental model
We set a fair value (FV) — our estimate of what the business is worth per share. Then we build a ladder: pre-decided price levels above FV where we trim a set amount. The ladder is mechanical on purpose — it takes the emotion out. When price hits a rung, we run a quick sanity check (the "four gates" in §4) and act.

02 "Trimming into a squeeze" — decoded

Two pieces of jargon in three words. Let's split them.

What's a "short squeeze"?

Some traders short a stock — they bet it will fall by borrowing shares, selling them, and hoping to buy back cheaper. If the stock instead rises, those shorts start losing money and rush to buy shares back to cap their losses. That buying pushes the price up even more, forcing more shorts to buy — a self-feeding spike. That's a squeeze. Key point: the price is rising because of forced buying, not because the business got better.

So "trimming into a squeeze" means…

Selling a slice while that artificial spike is lifting the price — handing your shares to the panicked buyers at an inflated price. You're using their forced demand as your exit liquidity. The opposite (and the mistake) is holding through it hoping it goes higher, then watching it fall back once the squeeze burns out.

Why it was the right call for RBRK
RBRK jumped ~5% in a day on heavy short interest and options-driven buying — no new business news. Selling into that = taking a good price the crowd handed us, not chasing a story. "A discipline that fires at your level but you defer hoping for more is a discipline you don't actually run."

03 The measuring sticks

Before the ratios: you need to know what goes on the top and bottom. These are the raw ingredients.

Market Cap vs. Enterprise Value (EV)

Market cap = share price × number of shares. It's the price of the equity alone. But if you were buying the whole company, you'd also inherit its debt and get to keep its cash. Enterprise Value fixes that:

EV = Market Cap + Debt − Cash

RBRK has more cash than debt (net cash), so its EV is a touch below its market cap. We use EV (not market cap) on the top of most ratios because it's the true "all-in" cost of owning the business.

The four things you can measure a company by

Going from "biggest / least honest" to "smallest / hardest to fake":

MetricPlain meaningHow gameable?
Revenue (Sales)Total money coming in the door.Hard to fake, but says nothing about profit.
EBITDAProfit before interest, taxes, and accounting write-downs (depreciation/amortization). A rough "operating cash engine."Flattering — ignores real costs. Trust it least.
Free Cash Flow (FCF)Actual cash left after running the business and paying for equipment/software. Money you could put in your pocket.Hardest to fake. Trust it most.
Earnings (Net income / EPS)Official accounting profit, per share. GAAP = strict rules; "non-GAAP" = company's adjusted version.GAAP honest but noisy for young firms; non-GAAP flatters.
EBITDA — why you asked, why we rarely lean on it
EBITDA is popular because it makes almost any company look profitable (you strip out the ugly parts). For a software company that pays people in stock, EBITDA can hide huge real costs. That's why for RBRK we lean on FCF and normalized earnings instead — they're the honest cousins. EBITDA is a "quick gut-check," not a verdict.
"Normalized" earnings — the key trick
Fast-growing companies spend so much to grow that they show little/no profit today, so a normal P/E looks absurd (hundreds ×). Normalizing = "assume the company is grown-up and spending like a mature peer — what profit margin would it earn then?" Apply that mature margin to today's revenue → a sensible earnings number you can value. It answers "what's this worth if it stops sprinting and starts printing?"

04 The four gates

A "gate" is just a valuation ratio used as a checkpoint: price ÷ some measure of the business. Low = cheap, high = expensive. Each gate looks at value from a different angle so one blind spot can't fool us.

① EV / Sales
RBRK ≈ 11.8×  rich-ish
In plain words
How many years of revenue you're paying to own the business. "I'm paying 11.8× a single year's sales."
Formula
EV ÷ annual revenue
Why it matters
Works even when a company has no profit yet — revenue always exists. Best for young, fast growers.
Cheap vs. dear
For a ~25%-growth software firm, ~10–15× is the normal "peer band." Under ~8× is a bargain; above ~15× is pricey.
RBRK
cheap · 6×fair · 11×expensive · 16×
RBRK: EV $19.4B ÷ FY27 revenue $1.64B = 11.8× → above our ~10.3× "fair" mark; no longer the bargain it once was. This is the weakest red flag — "fairly priced," not egregious.
② EV / EBITDA (gut-check cousin)
context only
In plain words
Price vs. the rough operating cash engine, before the ugly deductions.
Formula
EV ÷ EBITDA
Why we treat it lightly
For stock-heavy software firms EBITDA overstates real profit, so a "good" EV/EBITDA can be misleading. We show it for context, then defer to FCF.
We don't gate on EV/EBITDA for RBRK — its EBITDA is distorted by stock-based pay. FCF (gate ③) is the honest version of the same question.
③ EV / FCF
RBRK ≈ 65×  rich
In plain words
How many years of real, spendable cash you're paying. The honest profitability gate.
Formula
EV ÷ annual free cash flow
Why it matters
FCF is the hardest number to fake. If EV/FCF is high but EV/Sales looks OK, you're paying up for future profit that hasn't shown up yet.
Cheap vs. dear
Rule of thumb: a fair multiple is roughly the growth rate. ~25% cash growth ≈ a fair ~25–35×. 65× is well above that.
RBRK
cheap · 25×fair · 40×expensive · 70×
RBRK: EV $19.4B ÷ FY27 FCF ~$0.30B = ~65× (≈59× if the company beats guidance as it usually does). Paying a premium for cash that mostly hasn't arrived yet.
④ P/E and Normalized P/E
norm. ≈ 55×  rich
In plain words
Price ÷ profit per share. How many years of earnings you're paying. The everyday "how expensive" number.
Formula
share price ÷ earnings per share
The catch
RBRK barely earns a GAAP profit today, so its raw P/E is a meaningless ~300×. We use normalized EPS (mature-margin assumption, see §3) → a sensible ~55×.
Cheap vs. dear
A high-quality 25% grower is worth roughly a 35–40× normalized P/E. 55× is above fair — you're pre-paying for the growth.
RBRK
cheap · 30×fair · 38×expensive · 55×+
RBRK: revenue $1.64B × ~22% mature margin ≈ $0.36B normalized earnings → ~$1.58/share → at ~$88 that's ~55×. This is the method that set our $75 fair value, so any price well above $75 shows red here by design.
⑤ PEG — the "is the growth worth it?" gate
RBRK ≈ 2.2  expensive
In plain words
A P/E means less without knowing how fast the company grows. PEG divides the P/E by the growth rate to make fast and slow growers comparable.
Formula
P/E ÷ annual growth %
The reading
~1.0 = fairly priced  1–1.5 = getting rich  >2 = expensive
Nuance
Same idea can run on sales: sales-PEG ≈ 0.47 for RBRK (looks fine). The richness shows up on earnings/cash, not the top line — i.e. the growth is real, but you're overpaying for how it turns into profit.
RBRK: normalized P/E ~55 ÷ growth ~25 = ~2.2. Earnings-PEG says expensive; sales-PEG says the growth itself is still healthy. Both true — you're paying forward for the profit conversion.

05 Why require all four to flag

Any single ratio has a blind spot. EV/Sales can look fine while the company burns cash. P/E can be meaningless for a young firm. So the rule is a gate, not a trigger:

  • All four flag "expensive" → trim. Four different angles agreeing is hard to argue with.
  • Only one or two flag → pause and recheck. Probably a quirk of one metric, not real overvaluation.
  • Price hits the ladder rung but gates disagree → wait. The rung gets you to look; the gates decide.
RBRK verdict on the day
4 out of 4 flagged expensive (EV/Sales the mildest, the other three clearly). Gate satisfied → the trim was validated by the numbers, not just the price level. Backing it up: business signals were quietly softening (growth decelerating, the cash-collection metric halved year-over-year, the AI catalyst still unproven) — so we were selling strength into weakness-underneath.

07 The anchor & the ladder

Two last terms that tie it together:

Anchor / Fair Value (FV)
Our estimate of what one share is truly worth, from the gates converging. For RBRK: $75. It only moves when the business fundamentally changes (a new proven catalyst, or a new threat).
Trim ladder
Pre-set prices above FV where we sell a set slice. E.g. +18% → trim a quarter; +48% → trim another quarter. Mechanical, so a good price never gets talked away.
Weight-cap ladder
A second ladder that trims whenever the position grows too big a share of the whole portfolio — risk control, separate from valuation.
"Whichever is more aggressive wins"
When both ladders say trim, follow the one that sells more. Keeps you disciplined on both value and risk.

08 Quick glossary — the one-liners

EV
Enterprise Value = market cap + debt − cash. The all-in cost to own the whole business.
Revenue / Sales
Total money coming in. Says nothing about profit.
EBITDA
Profit before interest, tax, and write-downs. Flattering; a gut-check, not a verdict.
FCF
Free Cash Flow — real cash left after all spending. Hardest to fake.
EPS
Earnings per share — accounting profit divided by shares.
GAAP vs non-GAAP
Strict official rules vs the company's own adjusted (rosier) version.
Normalized
"What if it were mature?" — apply a grown-up profit margin to today's sales.
P/E
Price ÷ earnings per share. Years of profit you're paying.
PEG
P/E ÷ growth %. ~1 fair, >2 expensive. Makes fast & slow growers comparable.
Multiple
Any "× " ratio (11.8×, 55×). Shorthand for how many years you're paying.
Short squeeze
Forced buying by losing short-sellers spikes the price — not a business signal.
Trim
Sell a slice, keep the rest. Between "hold everything" and "sell out."
Peer band
The normal range of a multiple for similar companies. Context for cheap/dear.
Sandbag
Management lowballing guidance so they can "beat" it. We adjust for it.
