Part 1 · The foundation

How a language model actually works

Before we can talk about a hidden “workspace” inside Claude, we need a rough picture of what’s inside at all. Three ideas do the job: words become numbers, the numbers flow through an assembly line, and most of what happens on that line is invisible.

1. Words become numbers

A language model can’t manipulate letters directly. The first thing it does is chop your text into small pieces called tokens (roughly words or word-fragments) and turn each one into a long list of numbers — a few thousand of them per token.

That list of numbers is the model’s way of representing meaning. Think of it as a set of coordinates in a giant space, where words that mean similar things end up near each other. “Paris,” “London,” and “Tokyo” cluster in one neighborhood; “happy” and “joyful” in another. Nothing about the word “Paris” is stored as letters — it’s stored as a location.

◆ Key idea

Inside the model, a concept isn’t a word — it’s a pattern of numbers. Researchers call such a pattern a representation. Almost everything in this guide is about reading and editing these internal patterns. Hold onto that word: representation.

2. The assembly line of “layers”

Once your prompt is a set of number-patterns, it flows through a long stack of processing stages called layers — often dozens of them, stacked one on top of the next. This is what the “deep” in “deep learning” refers to.

Each layer does a small amount of work and passes an updated set of patterns to the next. Early layers handle surface-level things (spelling, basic grammar). Middle layers start assembling meaning and relationships. Later layers get closer to a decision about the next word to output. It’s an assembly line: raw parts go in one end, and a finished prediction — the next token — comes out the other.

3. Most of the work never reaches the page

Here’s the part that matters most for the rest of this guide. What you see from a model is only its output: the words it writes. But between reading your prompt and writing each word, an enormous amount of internal activity happens across all those layers — far more than ever shows up in the text.

Some of that hidden activity is bookkeeping (grammar, formatting). But some of it is genuine thinking: weighing options, working out intermediate steps, noticing things. The model can figure something out internally and simply never mention it.

▸ Concrete example

Ask the model, “How many legs does the animal that spins webs have?” To answer “8,” it first has to realize the animal is a spider — but the word “spider” appears nowhere in your question or in its answer. That step happened silently, inside the layers. Remember this example; it comes back as an actual experiment in Part 4.

You might have heard of “chain of thought” — when a model writes out its reasoning step by step. That’s useful, but it’s still output: words on the page. The hidden activity we’re talking about is different. It happens in the numbers, silently, whether or not the model writes anything down.

If you could somehow read those hidden number-patterns, you’d be reading the model’s thoughts — not its words. That is exactly what Anthropic figured out how to do.

Where we’ve gotten to

  • Representation — a pattern of numbers inside the model that stands for a concept.
  • Layers — the stacked stages the patterns flow through, getting refined at each step.
  • Hidden activity — the model thinks in these internal patterns, and most of that thinking never appears in its output.

That’s the whole machine, in three ideas. Next we’ll leave computers for a moment and look at the human-brain idea that inspired the discovery — because the researchers were deliberately hunting for something in Claude that mirrors how our minds separate the conscious from the automatic.
