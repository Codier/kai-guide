Part 2 · The borrowed idea

Conscious, unconscious, and the “global workspace”

Anthropic didn’t stumble on the J-space by accident. They went looking for it — guided by a decades-old theory from neuroscience about how your mind separates the thoughts you’re aware of from the ones you’re not.

The two kinds of mental activity

Right now, as you read this, your brain is doing a staggering amount of work you have no awareness of: adjusting your posture, controlling your breathing, turning shapes of ink into words. You can’t feel any of it happening. It’s unconscious.

But a thin sliver of your mental life is available to you — an image that pops into your head, a decision about where to go for lunch. Philosophers call this consciously accessible activity, and it has three telltale properties:

◆ What “consciously accessible” means
  • You can report it. If someone asks what you’re thinking about, you can say.
  • You can control it. You can choose to picture a beach, or do a sum in your head.
  • You can reason with it. You can chain these thoughts together to solve a problem deliberately.

Everything else — the grammar-parsing, the balance-keeping — runs automatically and silently. You can’t describe it, you can’t steer it, and you don’t reason with it. It just works.

Global workspace theory

The leading scientific attempt to explain why some activity is accessible and most isn’t is called global workspace theory (developed by Bernard Baars, and later Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues). Its picture of the mind goes like this:

Your brain is a big collection of specialist systems — one for vision, one for language, one for movement, and so on. They mostly work in parallel, unconsciously, and in isolation from each other. None of them can see what the others are doing.

A piece of information becomes consciously accessible at the moment it wins entry to a small, shared channel — the workspace — which is then broadcast to all the other systems at once. Suddenly every specialist can see that information and use it. That broadcast is what we experience as “being aware” of something.

◆ Key idea

The workspace is a bottleneck by design. It’s small, only a few things fit at once, and its whole value is that it’s shared: information written into it once can be picked up by every other system. That “write once, read anywhere” property is the signature of a workspace — keep it in mind for Part 4.

The five-point checklist

Put together, the theory says a genuine workspace should show five functional signatures. Anthropic turned these into a checklist they could actually test on Claude:

  1. Reportable — its contents can be described.
  2. Controllable — you can deliberately put things into it.
  3. Used for reasoning — multi-step thinking runs through it.
  4. Flexible & shared — one item in it can feed many different tasks.
  5. Selective — most processing stays automatic and never touches it.
The bet: if Claude has anything resembling a global workspace, it should pass all five tests. Part 4 shows it does.

One caution before we go on. Global workspace theory is a theory about function — about which thoughts are reportable, controllable, and shareable. It is not a claim that having a workspace makes you conscious in the felt, subjective sense. We’ll return to that distinction carefully in Part 5. For now, treat “conscious access” as a purely functional label.
