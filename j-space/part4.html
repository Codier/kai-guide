Part 4 · The proof

Five things the J-space can do

A word showing up in the readout could just be a coincidence — a scoreboard reflecting a decision made elsewhere. So the team ran the five-point checklist from Part 2. The star technique throughout is the swap: reach in, replace one word-pattern with another, and see if Claude’s behavior follows.

◆ The logic of the swap

If the J-space were just a passive scoreboard, editing it would change nothing — Claude would give the same answer. If Claude’s answer follows the edit, then the answer is genuinely being read out of the J-space. This single move is what turns “interesting correlation” into “this is causally doing the work.”

① It’s reportable

They asked Claude to silently think of a sport and then name it. Reading the J-lens right before it answered, Soccer sat at the top of the list — and sure enough, Claude said “soccer.”

Correlation so far. So they did the swap: reached in, removed the “Soccer” pattern, dropped in an equally strong “Rugby” pattern, left everything else untouched.

think of a sport, then name it → J-space: Soccer  →  Claude says “soccer”
now swap Soccer → Rugby in the J-space → Claude says “rugby”

The report followed the edit. And it works from the outside too: while Claude was reading a question, they injected a lightning pattern into its J-space, then asked what it noticed — Claude reported that the injected thought was about lightning. The J-space is where the report comes from.

② It’s controllable

Can Claude deliberately put things into its workspace, the way you can choose to picture a beach? They told Claude to concentrate on citrus fruits while copying out an unrelated sentence about a painting. Its output was just the painting sentence — but inside:

copying a sentence about a painting, told to hold “citrus” in mind →
J-space: orange, fruits, and words describing the act itself — thinking, imagery

They even had it do mental arithmetic while copying: asked to work out 3² − 2, the J-space showed nine and then, a few layers later, seven — the calculation happening entirely in its head, with nothing but the painting sentence on the page.

▸ The white-bear effect

Control isn’t perfect — and it fails in a very human way. When told not to think about something, the concept still lit up (less than when told to think about it, but far more than when it was never mentioned) — exactly the classic “try not to think about a white bear” problem in people. And when control slipped, the words damn and failure would appear in the J-space, as if Claude noticed its own lapse.

③ It reasons with it

Here’s the spider from Part 1, now as a real experiment. Prompt: “The number of legs on the animal that spins webs is…” To answer, Claude must first work out the animal is a spider — a word that never appears in the prompt or the answer (“8”).

“…animal that spins webs…” → J-space lights up spider mid-processing → answer “8”
swap spider → ant → answer becomes “6”

The second step of the reasoning took its input straight from the J-space and went along with whatever was put there. Same story with poetry: when Claude writes a rhyming couplet, it picks the rhyme word in advance and parks it in the J-space at the start of the line — swap that word and the whole line reorganizes around the new rhyme. The J-space isn’t watching the reasoning; it’s carrying it.

④ It’s flexible and shared

This is the experiment that most makes it a workspace rather than just a notepad. They gave Claude four different questions about France — its capital, its language, its continent, its currency — and made the exact same single edit in each: swap France → China in the J-space.

one identical swap, France → China, feeding four separate questions:
capital → Beijing  ·  language → Chinese  ·  continent → Asia  ·  currency → Yuan

All four answers changed together. If Claude kept a separate copy of “the country” for each kind of question, one edit could only have hit one of them. The fact that they all moved means they’re all reading from the same shared representation — which is the whole point of a workspace: write information in once, and many different systems use it.

◆ Wired like a broadcasting hub

Why can one pattern serve so many tasks? The team measured how connected each pattern is to the rest of the network. J-space patterns stood out dramatically — in some parts of the network, about 100× more components read from and write to them than to ordinary patterns. That’s exactly the wiring you’d expect of a broadcast hub: many systems post to it, many others pick up from it. This is the concrete, physical version of the “broadcast” idea from Part 2.

⑤ It’s selective — most thinking skips it entirely

A real workspace should be a small bottleneck, not the whole mind. And it is: the J-space holds only a few dozen concepts at a time and accounts for less than a tenth of Claude’s internal activity. So what happens if you remove it?

They deleted the J-space — stripped out its most active contents at every point — and watched what Claude could still do:

The cleanest demonstration used a Spanish passage. They swapped Spanish → French in the J-space and gave three tasks:

name the language → “French” (changed)
name a famous author in it → García Márquez → Victor Hugo (changed)
just continue the passage → still fluent Spanish (unaffected)

Naming the language or reasoning about it consults the J-space, so the swap changed them. But continuing to write Spanish — a skill practiced on enormous amounts of text — runs automatically and ignored the swap entirely. Just like you can speak grammatically all day without ever thinking about grammar.

Five for five. Reportable, controllable, reasoned-with, shared, and selective — the exact signature global workspace theory predicts.
